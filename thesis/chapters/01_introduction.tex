\section{Introduction and Experimental Objective}
\label{sec:intro}

Even at rest, the interval between two consecutive heartbeats changes from one beat to the next. In healthy people these changes are correlated across time scales, so that the pattern of fluctuation over a few beats resembles the pattern over minutes and hours \citep{peng1995,goldberger2002}. This fractal organisation is thought to reflect the interplay of many regulatory loops acting on the sinoatrial node at once, and it degrades in characteristic ways with disease and ageing \citep{lipsitz1992,goldberger2002}. The beat-to-beat timing of the heart therefore offers a continuous, non-invasive readout of how the nervous system is regulating the body.

Heart rate variability is the measure of choice here for practical and theoretical reasons. It is recorded beat by beat with a chest strap that tolerates movement. It can therefore follow a person through a movement practice that would be impossible inside a scanner or under an EEG cap. It reflects the output of a central autonomic network whose cortical nodes overlap with those of attention and self-regulation \citep{benarroch1993,thayer2000}, which makes it a bodily trace of central regulation. And it is already measured by consumer wearables, so any reliable relation between a practice and HRV can be carried out of the laboratory into everyday use.

Most research on heart rate variability (HRV) quantifies how much the heartbeat varies, using indices such as the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD). These amplitude indices are informative at rest but collapse towards a floor as soon as physical activity rises, losing resolution exactly where a movement task places the participant \citep{gronwald2020biomarker}. The short-term scaling exponent of detrended fluctuation analysis (\aone) quantifies how this variation is organised over windows of 4 to 16 beats. \al{} sits near 1.0 in rested, healthy adults, falls towards 0.75 around the first ventilatory threshold and towards 0.5 at high exercise intensity \citep{rogers2021threshold,rogers2022update}. Because it keeps a dynamic range under load and can be estimated from about two minutes of data \citep{chen2002,rogers2022update}, it is a candidate marker for short bouts of physically light but attentionally demanding movement.

One such class of movement is skilled object manipulation, such as juggling, poi, staff and stick work, which are widely practised and which practitioners describe as absorbing, calming and restorative, often in the vocabulary of flow \citep{csikszentmihalyi1990}. In cognitive neuroscience, object manipulation already serves as a model of skill learning, since learning to juggle changes grey and white matter within weeks to months \citep{draganski2004,scholz2009}. The state of the body during manipulation itself has received far less attention.

These practices belong to a community usually called the \emph{flow arts}. Here, props such as poi, staffs, hoops and sticks are practised as a movement discipline, a performance art and, for many, a contemplative practice. The present work grows out of seks.design, an experience-design practice that combines flow-arts movement with \emph{somatic journaling}, the structured recording of bodily sensations alongside reports of mental state. Its guiding idea, called here \emph{somatic interfacing}, is that signals from the body can serve as an interface for tracking, and eventually shaping, mental states. The longer-term aim is a model of conscious experience grounded in embodiment, in which movement, bodily signals and reported experience are mapped onto one another \confirm{wording of the seks.design description}. This thesis takes one step in that direction by asking whether a specific flow-arts movement leaves a measurable trace in cardiac regulation. If a practice reliably induces a distinct state of engagement, that state should leave a trace in autonomic regulation during the practice itself, and \al{} offers a way to look for it.

A second motivation comes from the study of rhythmic coordination. During walking, running and cycling, breathing tends to lock into stable frequency ratios with the movement cycle. An external rhythm such as a metronome can stabilise this locomotor--respiratory coupling and lower its energetic cost \citep{hoffmann2012,bardy2015}. Music and rhythmic sound, in turn, modulate arousal and can entrain cardiovascular rhythms \citep{bernardi2009,trappe2010}. A rhythmic upper-limb movement therefore offers a plausible route from movement to breathing to the heart, and one in which mechanical coupling plays a smaller role than in locomotion. Whether such coordination behaves like a skill, weak in novices and stable in experts, is the broader question that gives this thesis its title.

The present study uses an isolation stick, a prop of about 50~cm with a weight at each end that produces a rhythmic figure-eight motion. Isolation sticks were created more than 20 years ago by the French artist and manipulation specialist Alexandre ``Pich'' of Le Cirque Graphique. His design drew on Michael Moschen's work on rhythm and motion in object manipulation \todo{source for the history of the isolation stick}. Participants performed two families of movement with it. \emph{Isolations} keep one point of the stick fixed in space while the hand moves around it; they are slow, precise and visually guided. \emph{Spins} rotate the stick continuously around the hand; they are faster and more rhythmic. I recorded RR intervals with a Polar H10 chest strap and streamed them to a laptop. An open pipeline then computed \al, heart rate and amplitude indices of HRV for each two-minute trial. The control was a two-minute standing baseline in which participants held the stick still.

The experimental objective was to establish whether two minutes of stick manipulation shift \al{} relative to standing still with the stick, and in which direction. Because \al{} falls with exercise intensity, a shift could simply reflect physical load. A second objective was therefore to separate these two accounts by using mean heart rate as a covariate, so that an effect of manipulation counts as specific only if it exceeds what heart rate alone predicts. A third objective was to test whether the shift depends on the movement and on the participant's expertise. The same visible movement can be effortful and explicitly controlled in a novice and largely automatic in an experienced practitioner \citep{fitts1967}.

A further objective was to test the measurement pipeline itself. I built the capture system for this study from consumer hardware and open software. This makes it possible to compare the real-time \al{} reported by the streaming application with the estimate computed offline. Establishing how far these agree, and how robust \al{} is to the movement artifacts that object manipulation introduces, determines whether the stick can serve as a portable tool for studying states of skilled engagement outside the laboratory.

In summary, this thesis has three aims: (1) to test whether two minutes of stick manipulation shift \al{} relative to a standing baseline; (2) to test whether such a shift exceeds what the rise in heart rate predicts; and (3) to explore whether the shift depends on the movement and on expertise. In addition, it evaluates a portable recording pipeline that could support such measurements outside the laboratory.

The thesis is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews (1) cardiac autonomic control, (2) the method and interpretation of \aone, (3) acute changes in \al, (4) rhythmic coordination and the heart, (5) skill and flow, and (6) chest-strap recording. It closes with the hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes participants, design and the capture system; Section~\ref{sec:analysis} the offline signal processing and statistics. Sections~\ref{sec:results} to \ref{sec:conclusion} report, discuss and summarise the results.
