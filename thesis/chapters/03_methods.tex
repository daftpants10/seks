\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Participants}
\label{sec:participants}

I collected data in two phases. In the first phase (October 2026), five participants completed the full protocol; these recordings also served to check data quality and to fix the remaining protocol parameters. In the second phase (November and December 2026), about ten further participants were recruited. The sample consists mainly of experienced practitioners who can sustain both movements for two minutes, together with a small number of novices for comparison \confirm{final N per group and recruitment channels}. The target sample was therefore $N \approx 15$, of whom about three to five are novices.

Inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 45 years, no known cardiovascular, respiratory or neurological condition, and the ability to perform both movements with each hand for two minutes. Stick-naive participants received a short standardised instruction and practice period before recording (Section~\ref{sec:paradigm}). Exclusion criteria were arrhythmia, an implanted cardiac device, pregnancy, and medication known to affect heart rate or HRV, such as beta-blockers. Participants were asked to avoid caffeine for two hours and strenuous exercise for 24 hours before the session, and to avoid a heavy meal in the preceding two hours \citep{laborde2017}. Compliance with these requests and other known influences on HRV were recorded in a pre-session questionnaire (Section~\ref{sec:questionnaires}).

I assessed expertise in the same questionnaire with four items: (1) years of practice with the stick and with other flow-arts props; (2) practice hours per week; (3) a self-rating of proficiency for each movement from 1 to 10; and (4) whether the participant can sustain each movement for two minutes without dropping the stick. Participants who reported regular practice and could sustain both movements formed the experienced group; all others formed the novice group \confirm{grouping rule}. Because novices are few, expertise is analysed primarily as a continuous score derived from these items, and the contrast between groups is reported descriptively (Section~\ref{sec:stats}).

Recruiting mainly experienced practitioners follows from the research question. The primary comparison is within each participant, between a movement and that participant's own baseline, so expertise does not bias it. Experienced practitioners can sustain the movements for the full two minutes with few drops, which yields continuous movement bouts and cleaner recordings, and their data describe the effect of the movement as it is actually practised. Novices add a contrast, as they perform the same movement in an early, effortful stage of learning. The cost of this choice is generalisability, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

All participants gave written informed consent. \todo{ethics: name the approving committee or state that the study falls under the unit's existing approval; add reference number}

With a paired design, $N = 15$ provides 80\% power to detect a within-participant effect of $d \approx 0.78$ at $\alpha = .05$ (two-sided). Effects smaller than this, and all between-group comparisons of expertise, are therefore reported as estimates with confidence intervals.

\subsection{The Stick and the Movements}
\label{sec:stick}

The isolation stick was created more than 20 years ago by Alexandre ``Pich'', a French artist and manipulation specialist with Le Cirque Graphique, influenced by Michael Moschen's work on rhythm and motion \todo{source}. The stick used here is about 50~cm long with a weight at each end \confirm{exact length, mass and material}. The weighting moves the centre of mass outwards and gives the stick rotational inertia, so that once set in motion it tends to continue in a figure-eight path around the hand. The design comes from a movement practice that treats structured variability as the basis of skilled coordination: movement that keeps a recognisable pattern while varying slightly from cycle to cycle \confirm{whether to cite the EightOS / seks.design material here}.

I recorded two families of movement. In an \emph{isolation}, one point of the stick, typically its centre or one end, is held fixed in space while the hand and the rest of the stick move around it. Isolations are slow, demand continuous visual monitoring and fine force control, and break down visibly when control lapses. In a \emph{spin}, the stick rotates continuously around the hand or fingers in a figure-eight. Spins are faster, rely mainly on timing and momentum, and produce a regular rhythm once established. Each movement was performed with the left and with the right hand. Participants were instructed to keep the movement going for the whole trial at a pace they could sustain, to restart immediately after a drop, and to breathe naturally. Drops were counted by the experimenter.

\subsection{Experimental Paradigm}
\label{sec:paradigm}

The study used a within-participant design with one factor, \emph{condition}, at three levels (baseline, isolation, spin), and expertise as a between-participant variable, measured as a continuous score and summarised as novice or experienced. Hand (left, right) was recorded within each movement condition and treated as a secondary factor.

Because both movements are performed standing, the control condition was a standing baseline, during which participants stood still, holding the stick at rest in the hand, for the same duration as a movement trial. This baseline matches posture and grip, so the comparison between baseline and movement isolates the movement itself. A seated baseline would confound movement with posture, since moving from sitting to standing on its own lowers vagally mediated HRV \citep{laborde2017}. Each participant was their own control. The trial duration of two minutes was chosen as a compromise between two constraints. \al{} is conventionally estimated from a two-minute window (Section~\ref{sec:bg-dfa}), so shorter trials would not yield a stable estimate. Longer trials, in turn, exceed the duration for which isolations in particular can be sustained without fatigue of the forearm and repeated drops, which would turn the trial into a series of restarts. \confirm{2 min vs 3 min: if trials are extended to 3 min, \al{} is taken from the final 2 min so that the onset transient is excluded}

I recorded baselines with the same system and duration as the movement trials. Baseline and movement estimates are therefore based on comparable amounts of data. The pre-task baseline is the reference for H1; the post-task baseline serves the carry-over analysis.

\subsection{Technical Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Figure~\ref{fig:setup} gives an overview of the recording chain. I recorded RR intervals with a Polar H10 chest strap (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) worn at the level of the lower sternum with moistened electrodes. The H10 transmits RR intervals at a resolution of 1/1024~s through the standard Bluetooth Low Energy Heart Rate Measurement characteristic, in which a single notification can carry several RR values.

A first version of the setup connected the H10 directly to a browser page through the Web Bluetooth interface. On the laptop used for recording, this connection repeatedly dropped within seconds of being established, so the final setup routed the signal through a phone. I paired the H10 with the SomaSync application on an iPhone \confirm{app version}. The application keeps the Bluetooth connection stable and streams each RR interval, with its own real-time HRV metrics, over the local network.

A custom capture server (Node.js) running on the laptop received this stream. Its web interface, opened in a browser on the same laptop, allowed the experimenter to enter the participant identifier, the condition label and the trial duration before starting each trial. The server time-stamped each RR interval on arrival and stopped the trial automatically when the set duration had elapsed. Every trial therefore had the same length without manual timing. Each trial was saved as one file containing the RR intervals with timestamps and the trial details. A second file kept a raw log of every message received. Keeping the raw log means that any value reported by the streaming application can be recomputed or checked later. The code for the capture server and the analysis pipeline is available in the project repository \todo{repository link or archive DOI}.

\subsection{Real-time Metrics}
\label{sec:realtime}

In addition to the raw RR intervals, SomaSync reports its own running estimates of heart rate, RMSSD, SDNN, pNN50 and \al, which were stored with each trial. The settings behind these estimates (window length, detrending, artifact handling) are not documented in enough detail to be reproduced \confirm{check with SomaSync documentation or developer}. They were therefore not used for the primary analyses. Instead, they were compared with the offline estimates computed from the same RR intervals (Section~\ref{sec:stats}), which tests whether a consumer application can stand in for an offline pipeline in future, larger studies.
